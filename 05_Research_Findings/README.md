# Research Findings

## Regulatory Scope

The comparison identified three dimensions along which coverage differs: organizations covered, information protected and territorial applicability. Both frameworks assign responsibilities for protecting identifiable health information, but their coverage boundaries are not interchangeable. These findings reflect the October 31, 2026 baseline.

### Organizations Covered

HIPAA coverage centers on defined covered entities and business associates. Covered entities include health plans, health care clearinghouses and healthcare providers conducting specified electronic transactions. Business associates perform qualifying functions or services involving protected health information on behalf of covered entities, including through subcontracting relationships. GDPR assigns controller and processor roles across sectors, subject to its material and territorial scope. Handling health information alone therefore does not establish HIPAA coverage, while operating outside healthcare does not exclude GDPR coverage (45 CFR §§ 160.102–160.103; Regulation (EU) 2016/679, Articles 2–4).

### Information Protected

HIPAA protects individually identifiable health information within its coverage boundaries, subject to specified exclusions. Its Privacy Rule covers protected health information in different forms; its Security Rule addresses electronic protected health information. GDPR protects personal data within its material scope and treats health data as a special category. Article 2(1) covers wholly or partly automated processing and nonautomated processing of personal data that form part of, or are intended to form part of, a filing system. Coverage therefore depends on the information and processing context, rather than sensitivity alone (45 CFR § 160.103; Regulation (EU) 2016/679, Articles 2, 4 and 9).

### Territorial Applicability and International Transfers

HIPAA coverage follows covered-entity and business-associate relationships rather than an establishment or targeting test equivalent to GDPR Article 3. GDPR separately establishes territorial applicability through EU establishment and specified offering-of-goods-or-services or monitoring activities involving people in the EU. Identifying the applicable framework is therefore a separate decision from deciding whether information may be processed overseas (45 CFR §§ 160.102–160.103; Regulation (EU) 2016/679, Article 3).

The transfer comparison identifies an additional decision under GDPR. Where personal data are transferred to a third country or international organization, Chapter V requires an applicable transfer route, such as an adequacy decision, appropriate safeguards or a qualifying derogation. A processor agreement alone does not establish that route. HIPAA does not establish an equivalent Chapter V transfer mechanism. HHS guidance permits overseas cloud storage when applicable HIPAA requirements are met, including business-associate arrangements and assessment of geographic risks. Consequently, healthcare organizations and other entities processing health data may need both a valid service-provider arrangement and a separate GDPR transfer assessment for the same data flow (45 CFR §§ 164.308(a)(1)(ii)(A) and 164.502(e); Regulation (EU) 2016/679, Articles 44–49; U.S. Department of Health and Human Services, n.d.).

### Implication for the Research Question

Healthcare organizations and other entities processing health data must assess applicability separately under each framework. Shared safeguards can support governance, but cannot replace identification of the relevant organizational roles, information, activities and territorial conditions. Where processing involves an international transfer, satisfying HIPAA requirements does not itself satisfy GDPR Chapter V. These findings concern allocation of regulatory responsibilities rather than measured privacy, security or patient outcomes.

## Privacy Requirements

The comparison identified shared objectives in controlling health-information use, enabling individual rights and providing transparency. However, the legal pathways, conditions and procedures differ. These findings reflect the October 31, 2026 baseline.

### Permitted Use, Authorization and Consent

Both frameworks require a legal route for using health information. HIPAA establishes permitted uses and disclosures, including conditional pathways for treatment, payment and healthcare operations without individual authorization. Other activities may require authorization. Under GDPR, health-data processing requires an Article 6 lawful basis and an Article 9 condition. Consent is one possible route rather than a universal requirement. HIPAA authorization and GDPR consent should therefore not be treated as equivalent mechanisms (45 CFR §§ 164.502, 164.506 and 164.508; Regulation (EU) 2016/679, Articles 6–9).

### Purpose Limitation and Further Processing

GDPR Article 5(1)(b) links processing to specified, explicit and legitimate purposes and restricts incompatible further processing. Where Article 6(4)'s compatibility assessment applies, controllers consider the relationship between purposes, collection context, data sensitivity, consequences and safeguards. Compatibility is a distinct question from identifying the applicable lawful basis and health-data condition. HIPAA instead structures use and disclosure through its permissions, authorizations and associated conditions. A permission for one activity should not be treated as unrestricted permission for subsequent uses. This finding connects the regulatory comparison with Becker et al. (2023), who distinguish GDPR further processing from broader descriptions of secondary use (45 CFR §§ 164.502, 164.506 and 164.508; Regulation (EU) 2016/679, Articles 5(1)(b) and 6(4); Becker et al., 2023).

### Access, Correction and Portability

Both frameworks support access and correction, but their boundaries differ. HIPAA access concerns designated record sets; GDPR access includes personal data and prescribed processing information. At the cutoff, HIPAA generally required action within 30 days, with one permitted 30-day extension. GDPR required a response without undue delay and ordinarily within one month, with a conditional extension. HIPAA amendment procedures differ from GDPR rectification, and electronic access is not equivalent to the conditional GDPR portability right (45 CFR §§ 164.524–164.526; Regulation (EU) 2016/679, Articles 12, 15–16 and 20).

### Minimization, Retention and Erasure

Both frameworks limit information handling in relation to its purpose. HIPAA's minimum-necessary standard has specified exceptions, including certain treatment disclosures and requests. GDPR applies purpose-linked data minimization and provides conditional erasure rights. HIPAA provides no comparable general right to erase medical records. Its six-year documentation requirements are separate from medical-record retention duties under other laws (45 CFR §§ 164.502(b), 164.514(d), 164.316(b) and 164.530(j); Regulation (EU) 2016/679, Articles 5 and 17).

### Transparency, Restrictions and Disclosure Information

Notice requirements, restriction rights and recipient-information duties differ. HIPAA disclosure accounting has defined exclusions and a six-year lookback. GDPR recipient information forms part of its access framework; its interpretation includes the recipient-identification ruling in C-154/21. Marketing is subject to different authorization, lawful-processing and objection rules (45 CFR §§ 164.508, 164.520, 164.522 and 164.528; Regulation (EU) 2016/679, Articles 12–15, 18–19 and 21; RW v Österreichische Post AG, 2023, para. 51).

### De-identification and Automated Decisions

Information meeting HIPAA's Expert Determination or Safe Harbor requirements is no longer protected health information. This does not automatically establish GDPR anonymity. Under GDPR, pseudonymised information remains personal data where attribution remains possible; anonymous information is assessed under Recital 26. GDPR also addresses qualifying solely automated decisions with legal or similarly significant effects. The HIPAA provisions examined do not establish an equivalent general restriction (45 CFR §§ 164.502, 164.508, 164.514 and 164.524; Regulation (EU) 2016/679, Recital 26 and Articles 4(5) and 22).

### Implication for the Research Question

Healthcare organizations and other entities processing health data can coordinate notices, request handling and data-management procedures. Their processes must nevertheless preserve each framework's purpose, permission, rights, exception and timing requirements. A shared operational process does not establish that the underlying legal decisions are equivalent.

## Security Safeguards

HIPAA’s general security requirements address the confidentiality, integrity and availability of electronic protected health information, including protection against reasonably anticipated threats and impermissible uses or disclosures. GDPR establishes the integrity and confidentiality principle, supported by risk-appropriate security obligations. HIPAA identifies named safeguard standards and distinguishes required from addressable implementation specifications. GDPR Article 32 connects security measures to processing risks and context. These structural differences do not establish that either framework provides better protection (45 CFR § 164.306(a)–(b), (d); Regulation (EU) 2016/679, Articles 5(1)(f), 32).

The comparison examines seven safeguard areas: access controls, integrity controls, encryption and transmission security, audit controls, physical safeguards, recovery and workforce responsibilities. These findings reflect the October 31, 2026 baseline.

### Access Controls and Authentication

HIPAA requires technical access controls and verification of the identity of persons or entities seeking access to electronic protected health information. Unique user identification and emergency access procedures are required implementation specifications; automatic logoff is addressable. GDPR requires risk-appropriate security and steps to ensure that people acting under a controller’s or processor’s authority process data only on instructions, unless otherwise required by law. Both address unauthorized access, through differently structured obligations (45 CFR § 164.312(a), (d); Regulation (EU) 2016/679, Article 32(1)–(2), (4)).

### Integrity Controls

HIPAA requires policies and procedures protecting electronic protected health information against improper alteration or destruction. Its specification for mechanisms corroborating that information has not been altered or destroyed without authorization is addressable. GDPR expressly includes integrity within its security obligations. The comparison therefore identifies a shared protection objective, with different formulations of the associated duties (45 CFR § 164.312(c); Regulation (EU) 2016/679, Articles 5(1)(f), 32(1)(b)).

### Encryption and Transmission Security

Under the HIPAA baseline, encryption at rest and in transit is addressable. This requires assessment and implementation where reasonable and appropriate. A decision against implementation requires documented justification and consideration of a reasonable and appropriate equivalent alternative. Transmission security is a required standard; its integrity-control and encryption implementation specifications are addressable. Addressability therefore does not make transmission protection optional. GDPR includes encryption among measures to apply as appropriate to processing risks and context (45 CFR §§ 164.306(d)(3), 164.312(a)(2)(iv), 164.312(e)(1)–(2); Regulation (EU) 2016/679, Article 32(1)(a), (2)).

### Audit Controls and Activity Review

HIPAA expressly requires mechanisms to record and examine system activity, together with regular review of relevant activity records. GDPR requires accountability and regular assessment of security-measure effectiveness without prescribing the same universal audit-log specification in the cited provisions. System logging and evaluation of security effectiveness are related activities, but should not be treated as interchangeable (45 CFR §§ 164.312(b), 164.308(a)(1)(ii)(D); Regulation (EU) 2016/679, Articles 5(2), 32(1)(d)).

### Physical Safeguards and Device Handling

HIPAA identifies safeguards for facilities, workstations, devices and media, including disposal and reuse. GDPR addresses risks of unauthorized access, loss and destruction through its requirement for appropriate technical and organizational measures. Physical protection can support obligations under both frameworks, although their provisions organize these responsibilities differently (45 CFR § 164.310; Regulation (EU) 2016/679, Article 32(1)–(2)).

### Backups and Recovery

HIPAA specifies contingency-plan components, including required data-backup, disaster-recovery and emergency-mode operation plans. Testing and revision procedures are addressable. GDPR addresses ongoing availability and resilience, timely restoration after incidents and regular effectiveness testing. Both address continuity and recovery, while expressing the associated requirements differently (45 CFR § 164.308(a)(7); Regulation (EU) 2016/679, Article 32(1)(b)–(d)).

### Workforce Training and Sanctions

HIPAA requires security awareness and training, together with appropriate sanctions for workforce noncompliance with security policies and procedures. GDPR addresses processing under authority and, where a data protection officer is designated, includes awareness, training and related audits within that officer’s compliance-monitoring tasks. The cited GDPR provisions do not reproduce HIPAA’s internal-sanctions structure (45 CFR § 164.308(a)(1)(ii)(C), (a)(5); Regulation (EU) 2016/679, Articles 32(4), 39(1)(b)).

### Implication for the Research Question

The comparison indicates opportunities to coordinate security activities across the two frameworks. Organizations must nevertheless map those activities to each framework’s scope, implementation conditions and supporting evidence. Shared objectives do not establish interchangeable legal requirements. These findings describe regulatory responsibilities; they do not rank the frameworks or measure organizational compliance, security effectiveness or patient outcomes.

## Risk Management

The comparison identified six areas of risk governance: assessment, risk reduction, impact assessments, ongoing review, designated roles and documentation. Designated roles and documentation are included as accountability mechanisms through which risk-governance duties are assigned and evidenced. Both frameworks connect protective measures to identified risks. Their assessment scopes, procedural triggers and documentation requirements differ. These findings reflect the October 31, 2026 baseline.

### Risk Assessment

HIPAA requires an accurate and thorough assessment of potential risks and vulnerabilities to the confidentiality, integrity and availability of electronic protected health information held by regulated entities. GDPR connects compliance and security measures to processing context and risks to individuals’ rights and freedoms. The comparison identifies overlapping assessment activities, while distinguishing the legal scope of the risks examined (45 CFR §§ 164.308(a)(1)(ii)(A), 164.306(b); Regulation (EU) 2016/679, Articles 24(1), 32(1)–(2)).

### Risk Reduction

HIPAA requires security measures sufficient to reduce identified risks and vulnerabilities to a reasonable and appropriate level. GDPR requires risk-appropriate security and requires controllers to integrate data-protection safeguards into processing design and operation. Under both frameworks, identifying risks must be followed by implementing measures that address them. The applicable duties cannot be satisfied through assessment alone (45 CFR §§ 164.308(a)(1)(ii)(B), 164.306(b); Regulation (EU) 2016/679, Articles 25(1), 32(1)).

### Impact Assessments and Prior Consultation

HIPAA security risk analysis is a general requirement for Security Rule regulated entities. GDPR requires a data protection impact assessment before processing likely to result in high risk; large-scale processing of health data is a specified trigger. A DPIA examines necessity, proportionality, risks and safeguards. Prior consultation with the supervisory authority is required where the assessment identifies high risk that cannot be sufficiently mitigated before processing. A HIPAA risk analysis may contribute evidence to a DPIA, but does not automatically fulfil its requirements (45 CFR § 164.308(a)(1)(ii)(A); Regulation (EU) 2016/679, Articles 35(1)–(3), 35(7), 36(1)).

### Ongoing Review

HIPAA requires periodic technical and nontechnical evaluation, maintenance of security measures and updates to documentation following relevant changes. GDPR requires necessary compliance updates, regular security-effectiveness testing and DPIA review where necessary, at least when processing risks change. Neither cited baseline establishes a universal annual deadline for repeating every assessment. Review obligations must therefore be distinguished from annual intervals appearing in proposed reforms (45 CFR §§ 164.308(a)(8), 164.306(e), 164.316(b)(2)(iii); Regulation (EU) 2016/679, Articles 24(1), 32(1)(d), 35(11)).

### Governance Roles

HIPAA requires covered entities to designate a privacy official and complaint contact, while Security Rule regulated entities must identify a responsible security official. GDPR requires a data protection officer when its designation criteria apply, with defined position and task requirements. An EU representative is a separate role subject to its own conditions. These roles should be assessed individually rather than treated as equivalent appointments (45 CFR §§ 164.530(a), 164.308(a)(2); Regulation (EU) 2016/679, Articles 27, 37–39).

### Documentation and Records of Processing

HIPAA requires specified compliance documentation to be retained for six years from creation or the date it was last effective, whichever is later. GDPR prescribes controller and processor records of processing activities with different contents and conditional exemptions. Organization size alone does not establish the Article 30(5) exemption when processing includes health data. These documentation duties are distinct; HIPAA’s retention period is not a universal GDPR records-retention rule or a general medical-record retention period (45 CFR §§ 164.316, 164.530(j); Regulation (EU) 2016/679, Article 30(1)–(5)).

### Implication for the Research Question

The comparison indicates that organizations can coordinate risk identification, corrective actions, review schedules and supporting documentation. Each activity must nevertheless address the relevant assessment scope, procedural triggers and assigned responsibilities. Shared evidence may support work under both frameworks, while separate decisions remain necessary for DPIAs, prior consultation, role designation and records of processing. These findings describe governance requirements rather than their effectiveness in practice.

## Third-party Responsibilities

The comparison identified five areas of third-party responsibility: role allocation, contractual arrangements, downstream providers, vendor oversight and termination, and international transfers. Both frameworks assign obligations to organizations handling information for others. The applicable roles, contractual conditions and transfer requirements differ. These findings reflect the October 31, 2026 baseline.

### Roles and Responsibility Allocation

HIPAA defines business associates through specified functions or services involving protected health information on behalf of covered entities, including qualifying subcontractors. Business associates also have direct obligations under applicable HIPAA provisions. GDPR distinguishes controllers, processors and joint controllers according to their actual processing activities. A HIPAA business associate is therefore not automatically a GDPR processor; each role requires a separate assessment (45 CFR §§ 160.103, 164.502(e)(1); Regulation (EU) 2016/679, Articles 4(7)–(8), 26, 28(10)).

### Business Associate and Processing Agreements

HIPAA generally requires satisfactory assurances through a written business associate agreement or permitted arrangement, subject to regulatory exceptions. Required terms address permitted uses and disclosures, safeguards, reporting and assistance with individual rights. GDPR requires processor relationships to be governed by a binding written contract or qualifying legal act containing prescribed terms. Both establish contractual responsibilities, but an agreement meeting one framework’s requirements does not automatically meet the other’s (45 CFR §§ 164.502(e)(2), 164.504(e)(2)–(3), 164.308(b), 164.314(a); Regulation (EU) 2016/679, Article 28(3), (9)).

### Subcontractors and Subprocessors

HIPAA extends applicable restrictions and safeguards to subcontractors handling protected health information on behalf of business associates through required downstream assurances. GDPR requires prior specific or general written controller authorization for subprocessors. General authorization requires notice of intended additions or replacements and an opportunity to object. Required obligations flow down, while the initial processor remains fully liable to the controller for the subprocessor’s performance of those obligations. The comparison therefore identifies downstream protection under both frameworks, with distinct authorization and responsibility provisions (45 CFR §§ 164.502(e)(1)(ii), 164.504(e)(5), 164.308(b)(2); Regulation (EU) 2016/679, Article 28(2), (4)).

### Vendor Oversight and Termination

HIPAA requires reasonable steps to address known material violations of business associate obligations and termination where feasible if those steps are unsuccessful. Required contract terms address return or destruction of protected health information at termination where feasible, with continuing protections and limits where it is infeasible. GDPR requires selection of processors offering sufficient guarantees, compliance evidence and audit cooperation. At service completion, processors must return or delete personal data at the controller’s choice, including existing copies, unless applicable EU or Member State law requires storage. Ending a service relationship therefore does not itself establish that retained information has been appropriately handled (45 CFR § 164.504(e)(1)(ii)–(iii), (e)(2)(ii)(J); Regulation (EU) 2016/679, Article 28(1), (3)(g)–(h)).

### International Transfers

HIPAA does not establish a GDPR-style adequacy regime or a general requirement for US-only storage. Overseas service arrangements remain subject to applicable business associate agreements, safeguards and risk analysis, including consideration of geographic risks (45 CFR §§ 164.308(a)(1), 164.502(e), 164.504(e); U.S. Department of Health and Human Services, n.d.).

GDPR territorial applicability and international-transfer requirements are separate questions. EDPB Guidelines 05/2021 identify three cumulative transfer criteria: an exporter subject to GDPR for the relevant processing; disclosure or availability of personal data to a separate controller or processor; and an importer in a third country or an international organization. Direct collection from an individual in the EU by a US provider subject to Article 3(2) does not itself constitute such a transfer. Subsequent disclosure to a separate third-country provider may do so (European Data Protection Board, 2023, Section 2, pp. 7–9, Examples 1–2).

Where Chapter V applies, an applicable transfer route is required. The EU–US Data Privacy Framework adequacy decision covers transfers to qualifying US organizations on the DPF List, rather than all US recipients. Participation requires self-certification and eligibility under the recognized enforcement arrangements. FTC or Department of Transportation jurisdiction is therefore an important eligibility condition, separate from HIPAA coverage (Commission Implementing Decision (EU) 2023/1795, Article 1, recital 9 and Annex I, Section I.2).

This condition has implications for nonprofit healthcare organizations. The FTC generally lacks jurisdiction over most nonprofits, subject to exceptions (Commission Implementing Decision (EU) 2023/1795, Annex IV, footnote 2). A nonprofit hospital outside the recognized enforcement jurisdictions cannot itself rely on DPF certification as the recipient. Where Chapter V applies, another available route—such as applicable standard contractual clauses with the necessary assessment and safeguards—must be considered. Nonprofit status alone should not substitute for examining the particular entity and activity. This eligibility finding follows from the DPF decision and its accompanying FTC jurisdiction statement. Separately, Tschider et al. (2024) discuss healthcare-related limitations of the DPF and alternative contractual arrangements, including standard contractual clauses.

At the October 31, 2026 cutoff, the DPF adequacy decision remained operative. The General Court dismissed the challenge in Latombe v Commission on September 3, 2025; appeal C-703/25 P remained pending. The pending appeal should be distinguished from a judgment invalidating the decision (Latombe v Commission, T-553/23; C-703/25 P).

The comparison therefore identifies three distinct decisions: whether GDPR applies, whether the particular disclosure constitutes an international transfer, and which transfer route is available to the recipient. A HIPAA business associate agreement does not resolve those decisions.

### Implication for the Research Question

The comparison indicates opportunities to coordinate vendor inventories, contractual records, downstream-provider information and exit procedures. Organizations must nevertheless determine each party’s role, applicable agreement terms, authorization requirements and transfer conditions separately. Shared vendor-management activities can support both frameworks, while their underlying legal obligations remain distinct. These findings concern the allocation of responsibilities rather than measured vendor performance or compliance outcomes.
