# Discussion, Limitations and Conclusion

## Discussion

### Principal Findings and Allocation of Responsibility

This study compared 46 topics across eight domains of health-data privacy and security. HIPAA assigns responsibilities through defined covered-entity and business-associate relationships, while GDPR assigns controller and processor responsibilities according to processing activities across sectors, subject to its scope conditions. This difference affects which organizations, information and activities fall within each framework.

Within the safeguards examined, HIPAA identifies named standards and required or addressable implementation specifications. GDPR connects security measures to processing risks and context, while establishing rights and remedies—including conditional erasure, portability and compensation—that have no equivalent general counterpart in the HIPAA provisions examined. These are differences in legal structure, rather than evidence that one framework provides better protection.

Conduah et al. (2025) place healthcare privacy within a broader context of regulatory disparities, infrastructure constraints and organizational vulnerabilities. The present analysis narrows that context to the allocation and conditions of specific HIPAA and GDPR obligations.

The findings were broadly consistent with the provisional analytic expectations: security and risk-management activities shared objectives, while coverage, rights, transfers and remedies required distinct decisions. Breach notification differed in thresholds, recipients, starting events and exceptions as well as timing. This consistency is a qualitative interpretation of the selected documents, not a statistical confirmation of the expectations.

### Relationship to Existing Research

Becker et al. (2023) distinguish further processing under GDPR from broader descriptions of secondary use. Their analysis supports examining the purpose and organizational context of an activity rather than assuming that a label such as research establishes its legal conditions. The present comparison applies a similar distinction across privacy permissions, individual rights and governance responsibilities.

Tschider et al. (2024) examine the Data Privacy Framework’s implications for healthcare and discuss alternative contractual arrangements. Their work complements the finding that a transfer mechanism does not settle all conditions governing health-data processing. Processing permissions, organizational roles and applicable health-data requirements remain separate questions.

BRIDGE provides a particularly relevant comparator. Hou et al. (2026) used structured surveys and four Delphi meetings to develop a 30-step framework for transatlantic health-data collaboration. The present study complements that procedural emphasis through a source-linked comparison of obligations across eight domains at a defined historical cutoff. It does not claim to introduce the first HIPAA–GDPR comparison or to validate another collaboration framework.

### Shared Activities and Distinct Legal Decisions

The security, risk-management and third-party findings indicate opportunities to coordinate organizational evidence. A system inventory, access record, vendor register or recovery plan may inform work under both frameworks. Its relevance nevertheless depends on the obligation being assessed. A HIPAA security risk analysis does not automatically constitute a GDPR data protection impact assessment, and appointing a HIPAA official does not automatically satisfy GDPR’s data protection officer requirements.

Cornejo (2026) provides complementary incident evidence for examining vendor relationships. Business associates were involved in 281 of 831 provider-reported ransomware incidents in that study. These observations establish the relevance of third-party exposure, but do not demonstrate that maintaining a vendor inventory prevents breaches or that business-associate involvement invariably increases breach size. The organizational implication drawn here is to identify downstream relationships and the duties attached to them.

The distinction between an activity and its supporting legal decisions also applies to individual rights. A shared request-handling process may coordinate intake and communication while preserving different rules for access, amendment, portability and erasure. Likewise, common vendor oversight may support separate assessments of business-associate status, processor responsibilities and transfer conditions. Whether coordination reduces effort or improves compliance requires organizational evaluation.

### Cross-Border Healthcare and Recipient Eligibility

The international-transfer findings identify three sequential questions: whether GDPR applies, whether a particular disclosure constitutes an international transfer, and which transfer route is available to the recipient. Keeping these questions separate avoids treating either territorial applicability or HIPAA compliance as a complete answer to cross-border processing.

Under EDPB Guidelines 05/2021, direct collection from an individual in the EU by a US controller subject to Article 3(2) does not itself constitute a Chapter V transfer. Subsequent disclosure to another third-country organization may constitute a transfer. Organizations therefore need to examine individual disclosures and service relationships rather than classify an entire project as one EU–US data flow (European Data Protection Board, 2023).

Recipient eligibility introduces another distinction. DPF participation depends on its recognized enforcement arrangements. The FTC’s general exclusion of most nonprofits, subject to exceptions, means that HIPAA coverage and DPF eligibility cannot be assumed to coincide. A nonprofit healthcare recipient outside those arrangements must consider another available route where Chapter V applies. This is an implication of the eligibility rules, not a finding about how many hospitals are excluded or which mechanisms they use (Commission Implementing Decision (EU) 2023/1795, Annex I, Section I.2, and Annex IV, footnote 2).

### Incident Response, Remedies and Sanctions

Breach notification illustrates why coordinated investigation requires distinct reporting decisions. One investigation may generate evidence for both frameworks, while thresholds, recipients, discovery or awareness determinations and deadlines differ. Organizing a response solely around the shortest numerical deadline could overlook whether notification is required and who must receive it.

Zuiderveen Borgesius et al. (2023) explain notification through rationales including security incentives, regulatory oversight, transparency and accountability, while cautioning against unrealistic expectations about individuals’ ability to protect themselves after notice. Their analysis supports examining notification purposes and decision structures alongside timing, without inferring that a shorter deadline necessarily produces better outcomes.

Enforcement and penalties similarly require separation of regulatory complaints, corrective orders, private compensation, state-attorney-general damages and criminal proceedings. Jirsa and Horňáková (2026) provide complementary evidence from publicly reported healthcare GDPR enforcement decisions, describing variation in reported cases, fines and cited provisions. Their study also cautions against treating these records as direct measures of compliance or enforcement performance. Available legal powers, reported decisions and regulatory effectiveness are therefore distinct objects of analysis.

### How Selected Reforms Could Change the Comparison

The selected proposals would alter particular duties and implementation choices if adopted in the examined form. The HIPAA Security Rule proposal, PC1, would replace addressable encryption with an express requirement subject to limited exceptions, require multi-factor authentication with specified exceptions, and require procedures to restore critical relevant electronic information systems and data within 72 hours of loss. These changes would narrow specified implementation choices in the security domain, rather than eliminate all risk-based flexibility (90 FR 898, proposed §§ 164.308(a)(13)(ii)(D)(1), 164.312(b)(2)–(4), and 164.312(f)(2)(ii)–(iv)).

The examined Council Digital Omnibus negotiating text, PC3, would change GDPR authority notification from the existing risk-based threshold to a high-risk threshold and extend the feasible reporting period from 72 to 96 hours, while retaining the requirement to act without undue delay. This would change which breaches require authority notification and the associated reporting clock, without making those duties equivalent to HIPAA’s notification structure (ST 14756/26, Article 3(8), pp. 137–138).

These proposals would therefore change specific comparisons without making the frameworks interchangeable. Their effects remain conditional on the text ultimately adopted, application dates and transitional arrangements.

Regulation (EU) 2025/2518 represents a different status category. It was adopted and in force at the cutoff but applies from April 2, 2027, subject to transitional provisions. It is an adopted measure with deferred application, rather than a pending proposal or part of the enforcement procedures applicable on October 31, 2026 (Articles 36–37).

### Contribution and Further Evaluation

Taken together, the findings answer the research questions through responsibility allocation, conditional overlap in safeguards, distinct notification and remedy structures, and the duty-specific effects of selected reforms. The contribution is a comparison that retains those conditions alongside shared governance objectives.

Further evaluation could examine whether independent reviewers reach similar interpretations and whether representative healthcare scenarios reveal missing conditions. Organizational studies could then assess whether users distinguish shared activities from separate legal decisions and whether that distinction improves decision accuracy or administrative coordination. Those evaluations are necessary before presenting the comparison as a validated operational decision-support instrument.

## Limitations

This study uses comparative regulatory document analysis. It examines legal requirements and their organizational implications rather than how those requirements operate in practice. It does not measure compliance, implementation costs, security effectiveness, breach reduction, enforcement effectiveness or patient outcomes. Shared governance activities identified in the analysis should therefore be understood as opportunities for coordination, not demonstrated improvements in performance.

The legal scope is limited to federal HIPAA and EU-level GDPR requirements. US state law, Member State law, 42 CFR Part 2, the European Health Data Space and parallel reporting regimes—including national laws implementing NIS2 and the FTC Health Breach Notification Rule—are considered only where necessary to qualify an interpretation. They are not comprehensively compared. GDPR Article 9(4), for example, permits Member States to introduce further conditions concerning health data, including limitations. Consequently, the EU-level comparison does not establish all requirements applicable to a particular healthcare organization, research activity or data flow.

The findings reflect the legal baseline on October 31, 2026. Subsequent developments, including the November 2026 HIPAA Privacy final rule and the European Parliament’s Omnibus IV vote, may affect their practical relevance. Post-cutoff developments are distinguished from the historical baseline. Interpretations of pending proposals are conditional on the versions examined; the requirements ultimately adopted, their application dates and transitional arrangements may differ.

The analysis relied on English-language legal texts and accessible official materials, which may limit consideration of national interpretations and untranslated sources. Agency guidance informed interpretation but was not treated as binding legislation. Historical versions were assessed using dated captures and supporting official records; those materials remain subject to limitations in provenance and completeness. Court-affected provisions required separate examination of judgments and procedural status because the continued appearance of a provision in a regulatory text does not, by itself, establish its enforceability. These checks do not establish an exhaustive account of every potentially relevant legal development.

The comparison matrix and eight domains existed before the coding guide was formalized. The subsequent audit refined the analysis iteratively rather than constituting a prospectively specified independent coding exercise. The interpretations have not undergone independent legal review, which limits assurance that another reviewer would reach the same conclusions or identify the same exceptions. Topic counts describe the organization of the comparison; they are not independent observations and do not support statistical estimates of similarity, convergence or comparative effectiveness.

The literature review draws on a selected set of studies rather than an exhaustive systematic search. Its coverage limits the contribution claim: the study offers a source-linked comparison across the selected domains and cutoff, but does not establish that no equivalent comparison exists. Additional searching and specialist review may identify overlapping work or interpretations that require the analysis to be narrowed or revised.

## Conclusion

This comparative document analysis examined 46 topics across eight domains of health-data privacy and security under HIPAA and GDPR, using an October 31, 2026 baseline. HIPAA assigns responsibilities through defined covered-entity and business-associate relationships, while GDPR assigns responsibilities according to processing roles and activities within its scope. Shared protection objectives coexist with differences in coverage, individual rights, safeguards, third-party obligations, notification, remedies and sanctions.

The central organizational implication is to distinguish shared governance activities from the legal decisions they support. Organizations may coordinate risk assessments, vendor records, incident investigations and supporting documentation, while separately determining each framework’s applicability, conditions and required actions. Cross-border processing particularly requires distinguishing whether GDPR applies, whether a disclosure constitutes an international transfer and which transfer route is available to the recipient.

The study contributes a source-linked account of these distinctions while separating applicable requirements, pending proposals and adopted measures with deferred application. If adopted in the examined form, selected reforms would alter specific duties—notably HIPAA encryption, authentication and recovery requirements, and GDPR authority-notification thresholds and timing—without making the frameworks interchangeable. The study does not rank the frameworks or establish their effectiveness in practice. Independent review and evaluation in representative healthcare settings are needed before the comparison can be presented as a validated operational decision-support instrument.
